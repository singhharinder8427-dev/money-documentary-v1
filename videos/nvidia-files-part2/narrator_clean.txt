Monday, November sixteenth. A reporter asks the President whether his Secretary of State warned him.
The Washington Post says it came from the Secretary of State. A warning about Nvidia's CEO.
The President's answer is short. "I haven't heard that."
But reports say he was warned twice. Once last fall. Once in May.
One company in this story bought more than four point eight billion dollars of Nvidia chips.
And whatever Washington decides about those chips lands in your fund.
So who knew? And what did they do about it?
Rewind to spring 2026.
According to the New York Times, a highly classified memo starts moving through Washington.
It says Alice Huang was recruited by China's Ministry of State Security. To gather intelligence on Jensen Huang.
By then, Singapore had already opened its own investigation into her company. Its police impounded her passport in June 2025. Singapore says that probe was about false declarations on server purchases. Not espionage.
Remember what we know. She has not been charged. Beijing calls it groundless. The Justice Department has reached no conclusion.
The memo, reportedly, was meant to help officials who deal with Huang know what they were dealing with.
The memo was not the first warning. If reports are right, the first came months earlier.
Last fall. Reports say President Trump was briefed on intelligence about Alice Huang's closeness to Jensen Huang.
The New York Times reported he took no action.
By then, the two Huangs had been photographed together for almost two years. The first known photo is from a Taipei trade show in June 2024.
Then, May twelfth. Air Force One is headed to China.
ABC News reports Secretary of State Marco Rubio shared intelligence with the President in flight. It calls that the second known warning.
Jensen Huang joined that trip.
Hold that date. May twelfth.
Here is where the story gets stranger.
Axios reports that after the memo, top officials pressed Commerce to act. Treasury. Intelligence. The Pentagon.
Commerce runs export controls. Axios reports Secretary Lutnick's department resisted. Commerce denies it. Treasury's Bessent has said publicly that Commerce was slow. And the President says Lutnick might have delayed it.
Meanwhile, Bloomberg reported that Nvidia's head lawyer had intervened in a probe of Megaspeed. Then called a Commerce official to ask about Alice Huang's case.
Megaspeed says it has complied with United States law. Alice Huang left the company in August 2025, it says.
What nobody knew until October.
Jensen Huang told Bloomberg the government had never told him of its suspicions.
A memo. Two reported briefings to the President. Officials across Washington. Everyone, reportedly, but him.
Then it all came out within days.
November eighth. Huang tells Nvidia employees he was unfaithful to his wife. The board opens an independent investigation. It says it has no reason to believe national security was compromised.
November ninth. China's Foreign Ministry calls the spy claim groundless. The President says Huang told him by phone he had no idea.
November twelfth. Huang resigns. Democrats Neal and Wyden write to Lutnick, saying the administration did not appear to take the threat seriously.
Congress wants answers. House chairs asked for answers. Huang is due for a closed-door House interview on December first.
Monday the sixteenth. The President says he has not heard about Rubio's warning. He says he would be fine with Huang coming back.
By the seventeenth, the Wall Street Journal reported an SEC probe. Huang said he would be honored to return.
Here is where this reaches your money.
In December twenty twenty-five, the administration announced Nvidia could sell advanced H200 chips to China, in exchange for a twenty-five percent cut. No one has shown that the briefings and that rule are connected.
But it shows how much one government decision can move one company. And Nvidia is more than six hundred dollars of every ten thousand in a typical S&P 500 fund.
So here is a habit. When a company's growth depends on a government rule, the rule is part of what you own.
Ask three things. Which decisions can switch its biggest market on or off? Who checks its biggest customers? And who gets warned, and when?
Not so you can guess the next headline. So you know what you are actually holding when the headline lands.
