Rewind to 2023, in Singapore.

A little-known company called Megaspeed has just been reborn.

Before that, it was the Singapore arm of a Chinese video game company.

Within days of its sale to a Singapore company, reports say, it became an official Nvidia cloud partner.

Now it wanted to rent out computing power. The kind built on Nvidia chips.

Everyone wants those chips. Not everyone is allowed to have them.

Jensen Huang co-founded Nvidia in 1993. For thirty-three years, he was its only CEO.

Its boss was Alice Huang. A Chinese national, according to United States reports. A Hong Kong resident, according to Singapore.

No relation to Jensen Huang. Same last name. That is all.

In 2023, Megaspeed's assets were about thirty-three million dollars.

A year later, almost three billion.

So how do banned chips allegedly reach China? Three ways.

One. Smuggling. Prosecutors in a separate case say workers swapped labels with a hair dryer.

Two. Middlemen. A company in Southeast Asia buys the servers and quietly passes them on.

Three. Renting. The chips never leave Asia. A buyer in China just logs in from afar.

In May 2025, the Commerce Department warned United States firms to watch for partners offering cloud access to China.

Think of a pizza oven banned in one town. Nobody can sell you the oven. But anyone can sell you pizza.

Here is where this reaches your money.

You probably own Nvidia. Even if you have never bought a share.

If you hold an S&P 500 index fund, about six percent of it is Nvidia.

This spring it was closer to eight and a half. In 2022, it was about one.

That is roughly six hundred dollars of every ten thousand you invest.

When Nvidia dropped about eleven percent, the rest of the index cushioned it. This time.

A decade ago, the ten biggest companies were under a fifth of the S&P 500. Today, seven are about a third.

That is called concentration risk. And one person can move it.

Here are five things worth checking.

One. How much of your fund is just a few companies?

Two. Does one founder hold the whole company together?

Three. Does one customer or partner drive big growth?

Four. Can the biggest customers be checked? Or are they a mystery?

Five. Do the board and the rules move before the headlines do?

This is education, not financial advice. Just a habit: open your fund, and look inside the box.
